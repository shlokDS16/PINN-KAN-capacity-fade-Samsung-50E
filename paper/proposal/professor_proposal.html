Summary

Battery ageing campaigns are slow and expensive: every additional cycling condition costs months of test-channel time. I propose to measure how much testing a physics-informed neural network (PINN) can save. The model joins a calendar-ageing term (Arrhenius in temperature times a Kolmogorov–Arnold network, KAN, in state of charge) and a cycling term (a KAN over temperature, state-of-charge window, depth of discharge and C-rates, scaled by charge throughput).

On the public Samsung INR21700-50E campaign (279 cells), I will train with only 8, 16 or 32 cycling conditions and with 0, 2 or all early check-ups of each held-out cell. I then forecast the capacity fade of 40 cycling conditions the model never saw, and compare against the best tuned data-driven learner.

Two further questions isolate each component. Does the gain come from the physics law? I answer this by removing the law from the same network. Does the KAN give a written-out stress law that predicts unseen conditions as well as the full network, and better than an equally simplified additive model? The analysis plan, including the wording of every possible conclusion, is fixed before any test result is seen.

1. Problem and gap

PINN and KAN models for battery state of health are now common in the Journal of Energy Storage, including physics-guided and physics-informed KAN estimators (PIKAN, PGKAN). These papers estimate the health of cells whose own data the model has already seen. They do not show whether the physics saves ageing tests, and they cannot say which component produced the gain. They also do not check that an extracted symbolic law holds on conditions outside the training set.

The closest work on test economy is different in kind:

  • Lucu et al. measured the minimum number of laboratory tests a Gaussian-process ageing model needs, with no physics law and no ablation.
  • Stroebl et al. applied optimal experimental design to this same Samsung dataset. They calibrated a semi-empirical model and found that its cycle-ageing part was inadequate.
  • Zhang et al. trained an equivalent-circuit PINN on this dataset to recover circuit parameters. That work does not forecast capacity fade.

2. Research questions

  1. Data efficiency (PINN). With 8 cycling conditions and 2 check-ups per held-out cell, is the PINN's typical held-out-condition error lower than that of the best tuned data-driven learner (the better of a Gaussian process with automatic relevance determination, LightGBM and the same network without physics)? How does the gap change at 16 and 32 conditions?
  2. Attribution. How much of any advantage comes from the physics law? I test this against the same network without the law, and against the same law with an additive (GAM) stress map instead of the KAN.
  3. Readable law (KAN). Does a closed-form stress law extracted from the KAN predict held-out conditions no more than 10 % worse than the full network? Is it more accurate than an equally simplified additive law? Both are built with the same symbolic library, which is fixed in advance.

3. Data

  • Primary: Samsung INR21700-50E (Stroebl et al., Scientific Data 2024, CC BY 4.0). 279 cells in two stages from three laboratories. The calendar arm has 132 cells in 19 conditions and the cycling arm 147 cells in 40 conditions. Capacity comes from periodic check-ups. The full release (10.3 GB) is being downloaded with checksum verification.
  • Supporting: ISU-ILCC (251 cells, 63 cycling conditions) for a training-side pilot, and Sandia (61 cells) as a structural check.
  • Why not XJTU, TJU, MIT or HUST: these sets vary a few cycling protocols and have no storage arm, so they cannot separate calendar from cycle ageing or support a test of how many conditions are needed. Samsung's designed grid of storage and cycling conditions can.
  • Known limitation, stated in the paper: temperature is partly aliased with laboratory. No activation-energy or temperature-mechanism claim is made for the cycling term, and results are reported per laboratory.

4. Method

Model

The PINN has a solution network for capacity loss with a hard initial condition. It is trained on check-up data plus a physics residual that ties its time derivative to the ageing law. The law has two parts:

  • a calendar term, Arrhenius(T) × KAN(SOC);
  • a cycling term, a KAN over (T, SOC window, DoD, Cch, Cdch) times the throughput rate.

A per-cell offset is inferred causally from that cell's early check-ups only. All tuning (the same number of Bayesian-optimisation trials for every tuned model) uses training conditions only.

Experimental grid

The 40 held-out cycling conditions (5 folds of 8) are identical in every cell of the grid; only the training side is thinned. Training subsets are nested and chosen from stress coordinates alone, never from capacity data, with every cycling temperature represented at m = 8.

5. Evaluation and statistics

  • Unit: the held-out cycling condition (n = 40). The estimand is the median, over conditions, of the log ratio of forecast errors between two models.
  • Pre-registration: hypotheses, margins (2 % for superiority, 10 % for non-inferiority), the multiple-testing graph and every abstract sentence are written down and hashed before any test score exists. Code locks the test folds until that file is frozen.
  • Correlation-aware test: simulation showed that a plain sign test becomes too lenient when conditions in the same fold or laboratory share errors. The confirmatory test therefore uses a fixed design effect: 31 of 40 conditions must favour the PINN at the headline cell, instead of 28. Laboratories are fixed strata with per-laboratory results.
  • What this means for detectability (simulation, not a result): the PINN needs a true gain of about 21–25 % at g1 to have an 80 % chance of showing it. Smaller gains will be reported with their intervals, as inconclusive rather than negative.
  • Safeguards:
    • a label-invariance negative control in every run;
    • sensitivity rows that can only lower a claim: per laboratory, laboratory indicators, all check-ups retained, and random training subsets;
    • mean and worst-condition errors reported beside the median.

Realistic expectation: T2 (the PINN wins and the KAN law is readable) is the best plausible case. T5 (an honest learning-curve paper) is about as likely. Both are publishable in JES. The second is weaker, but it still answers a question test engineers ask.

7. Getting the strongest result honestly

The goal is the best result the method can earn, not the best-looking result. Before the plan is frozen, every lever that can raise the PINN's real performance is tuned on training data only. Every comparator gets the same care, because a weak baseline would make any win worthless to reviewers.

What we do before the freeze
  • An independent machine-learning review of the PINN for scarce data: priors and regularisation, loss weighting, per-cell offsets, tuning budget, ensembling, and use of the storage cells. Each change is applied equally to comparators where it applies.
  • A training-side pilot on ISU to decide the top changes by a rule fixed in advance.
  • A simulation-backed choice of how the error budget is split between the PINN and KAN questions. The decision rule is fixed now, and the split can raise the chance of the full PINN result from 0.36 to about 0.5 (simulation).
  • All check-ups of every cell from the full dataset download.
What we never do
  • Tune, select or stop on held-out conditions.
  • Change the metric, margins, headline cell or wording after seeing results.
  • Drop conditions, cells or laboratories that hurt the result.
  • Weaken a baseline, or give the PINN features or budget the baselines lack.

8. Expected contribution

  • A measured answer to a practical question for battery test planning: how many cycling conditions and check-ups a physics prior saves, with learning curves.
  • An attribution of any gain to the physics law versus the KAN, by a matched ablation ladder.
  • A closed-form stress law checked on conditions it never saw, against an equally simple additive law.
  • A fully reproducible release: code, frozen plan with its hash, splits and per-condition predictions (Zenodo DOI).
